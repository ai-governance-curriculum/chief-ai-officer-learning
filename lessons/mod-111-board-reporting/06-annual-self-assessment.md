# Chapter 6 — The Annual Program Self-Assessment

## Why this chapter exists

Once a year, the CAO function conducts a
self-assessment of the program. This is the
program's chance to survey itself — away from
incident pressure, outside the audit calendar,
without the quarterly reporting cadence — and
to put its own honest view of what is and is
not working on the record.

The self-assessment is not an internal audit.
Internal audit is the third line of defense
([`mod-101`](../mod-101-foundations/README.md)
§3) and is independent of the CAO function; its
view will reach the board separately through
the Audit Committee's direct relationship with
the Chief Audit Executive. The self-assessment
is the *CAO function's own* honest review of
itself.

The two views complement each other. Internal
audit is constrained by its methodology — the
auditors can only find what is within their
testing scope and documented procedures. The
self-assessment is constrained by self-interest
— the CAO reviewing their own function has
every incentive to over-rate it. A board that
sees both views, and sees them diverge in
specific ways, gets a more complete picture
than either view alone provides.

This chapter builds the structure that
produces an honest self-assessment — one
substantive enough that a peer CAO at a
comparable institution reading it would judge
it credible rather than self-serving.

## 6.1 What the self-assessment covers

A working self-assessment covers six
dimensions:

### 6.1.1 Program maturity

Rating against a defined maturity model. Three
anchors in common use:

- **NIST AI RMF maturity levels.** The AI RMF
  itself does not publish a maturity model;
  practitioner adaptations typically use five
  levels (Initial, Developing, Defined,
  Managed, Optimising) applied to each of the
  four RMF functions (GOVERN, MAP, MEASURE,
  MANAGE).
  <!-- needs-research: confirm current state of NIST AI RMF maturity companion material; a formal maturity companion may post-date this writing -->
- **ISO 42001 readiness.** The ISO 42001
  management-system clauses provide an
  implicit maturity progression — a program
  can be certified, substantially ready for
  certification, early in the readiness
  journey, or outside the ISO 42001 shape.
- **Sector-specific maturity benchmarks.** For
  banking: SR 11-7 compliance posture,
  OCC Heightened Standards attestation, PRA
  SS1/23 alignment where applicable. For
  insurance: NAIC Model Bulletin alignment.
  For healthcare: FDA SaMD alignment where
  applicable.

For each anchor, the rating is specific —
"GOVERN function at *Developing* (NIST level 2),
with specific evidence X, Y, Z" rather than
"the program is maturing." The gap to the next
level is named.

### 6.1.2 Operating effectiveness

Are the controls operating? What does the
evidence show? This dimension draws from the
compliance-operations continuous evidence
([`mod-109`](../mod-109-compliance-operations/README.md))
and the audit ledger ([`mod-108`](../mod-108-audit-ledgers-and-evidence/README.md)):

- Which controls were tested in the year.
- Which produced the expected evidence
  reliably.
- Which produced evidence inconsistently or
  not at all.
- Specific incidents (and near-misses) that
  tested specific controls — did the
  controls perform?

An operating-effectiveness section that reads
"all controls operating" is almost always
wrong; some controls will have gaps even in a
well-run program. The honest version names
them.

### 6.1.3 Coverage gaps

What is not yet covered that should be. Three
to five specific gaps, each with:

- The gap itself (what is not covered).
- Why it is not covered yet (resourcing,
  timing, dependencies).
- The risk the gap represents (what happens if
  it is not closed).
- The recommended path to closure.

Programs that produce coverage-gap sections
with zero gaps are either operating at a
maturity level very few programs reach or are
not surfacing honestly. Programs that produce
sections with twenty gaps have overloaded the
section; the discipline is to name the
material gaps and triage the rest to the AIRC
backlog.

### 6.1.4 Investment posture

Where the program invested in the year.
Where the program under-invested. Where the
year-ahead investment should focus.

Investment posture is a dimension boards
particularly care about because the CFO is
reading the same report. Specific numbers
matter here more than in other sections:
headcount by function, external-vendor spend
by category, infrastructure spend, year-over-
year change.

### 6.1.5 Leadership and talent

Does the CAO function have the right
capabilities? What gaps exist?

- Year's hiring outcomes — hires planned,
  hires made, retention.
- Capability gaps remaining — functions where
  bench depth is thin, functions where market
  competition for talent is making hiring
  slow.
- Talent risks — key person dependencies,
  succession posture for the CAO role
  itself, market compensation pressure.

Boards and Audit Committees take talent risk
seriously because it is the risk most likely
to produce a surprise failure. A single-threaded
function — one person who holds the
operational knowledge — is a specific finding
to make.

### 6.1.6 Cultural integration

Is the program integrated into the
organisation's operations or operating in
parallel? Evidence either way:

- Business lines that treat the AI program as
  a routine part of operations vs. business
  lines that treat it as a compliance box.
- Material decisions in-flight where the AI
  function was consulted substantively vs.
  consulted ceremonially.
- The reflex response from the business when
  an AI-related issue surfaces — do business
  teams reach for the AI function
  substantively or route to legal/compliance
  as a default?

Cultural integration is the dimension most
likely to surface uncomfortable findings. A
first-year CAO function that reports it is
fully culturally integrated is almost
certainly wrong; a third-year function that
reports no integration gains is almost
certainly not pushing hard enough.

## 6.2 The honesty test

The hardest part of the self-assessment is
honesty. A self-assessment that says
everything is working is rarely accurate; a
self-assessment that says nothing is working
is rarely accurate either. The right posture
is specific findings, both positive and
negative, with evidence.

A test — the one that most reliably surfaces
honest / dishonest patterns:

> *Would the CAO be comfortable showing the
> self-assessment to a peer CAO at a
> comparable institution who would give an
> honest read?*

If yes, the self-assessment is probably
honest. If the CAO would want to revise before
showing, the self-assessment is not there yet.

A corollary: a self-assessment that includes
no uncomfortable findings fails the test. Every
program at the end of its first full year has
specific gaps, specific under-investments,
specific moments where the function did not
perform at the standard it aspires to. Naming
them in the self-assessment is how the function
earns credibility to then name the specific
asks that follow.

## 6.3 The structure

A working self-assessment is **5–8 pages** and
has these elements, in this order:

- **Executive summary.** One page. The three
  substantive findings; the three substantive
  recommendations.
- **Program maturity.** §6.1.1 content, ~1
  page.
- **Operating effectiveness.** §6.1.2 content,
  ~1 page.
- **Coverage gaps.** §6.1.3 content, ~¾ page.
- **Investment posture.** §6.1.4 content, ~¾
  page.
- **Leadership and talent.** §6.1.5 content,
  ~½ page.
- **Cultural integration.** §6.1.6 content,
  ~½ page.
- **Specific recommendations.** The year-
  ahead action list, prioritised. ~¾ page.

The executive summary is read; the detail is
reference. The same discipline as the
quarterly report applies — the material at the
front, the depth available for anyone who
wants it.

## 6.4 The output

The self-assessment produces three artifacts:

### 6.4.1 The document

Written, signed by the CAO, submitted to the
AI Risk Council and the Audit Committee. The
signature matters: the CAO is personally
staking credibility on the specific findings.
A document that is drafted by a staff analyst,
reviewed by the CAO at the margin, and then
anonymously issued does not carry the same
weight — boards read a signed self-assessment
differently than they read an unsigned staff
product.

### 6.4.2 The action list

A specific list of what the program will do
differently in the year ahead. The actions
follow the same discipline as mod-110 post-
incident review recommendations — specific
owner, specific timeline, specific tracking,
no "we will consider" placeholders. The
action list is tracked across the year through
the quarterly reports; year-over-year, the
status of previous years' action lists
becomes a specific input to the current year's
assessment.

### 6.4.3 The resource request

What the CAO function needs to do the above.
The request is the input to the budget cycle
and to the operating-model dimensioning
([`mod-112`](../mod-112-cao-operating-model/README.md)).
A self-assessment without a resource request
has either an exceptionally resourced function
(rare) or a CAO who is not asking (common,
and worth correcting).

## 6.5 The annual cycle

A working annual cycle:

- **Q4 of the preceding year.** Self-
  assessment is authored. The CAO function
  gathers evidence, drafts the document,
  internally reviews it against the §6.2
  honesty test.
- **Q1.** Self-assessment is reviewed by the
  AI Risk Council. The Council's input shapes
  the final document; the Council also uses
  the recommendations as input to its own
  annual planning.
- **Q1.** Self-assessment is submitted to the
  Audit Committee. The committee may ask
  questions, request specific follow-up, or
  accept the assessment as submitted. The
  CFO and the CRO receive copies.
- **Q2–Q4.** Actions are tracked through the
  quarterly reports (Chapter 3). The
  quarterly report's §3.1.4 (material
  decisions pending) section carries any
  recommendations that require board
  ratification; the §3.1.2 (material
  changes) section carries recommendations
  closed in-quarter.

The self-assessment closes the loop on the
program's GOVERN function ([`mod-103`](../mod-103-ai-risk-frameworks/README.md)
§6) at the annual level, as the quarterly
report closes the loop at the quarterly level
and the post-incident review ([`mod-110`](../mod-110-incident-response/README.md)
§6) closes the loop at the incident level.

## 6.6 Common failure modes

Three patterns to recognise:

### 6.6.1 The marketing self-assessment

The document reads as if it were drafted for
external promotion of the function. Every
section emphasises successes; gaps are
reframed as opportunities; the maturity
ratings are generous. Boards read these as
marketing material and discount them.

The pattern tends to emerge when the CAO
function is new and the CAO feels pressure to
justify the function's existence. The remedy
is the §6.2 honesty test — showing the draft
to a peer CAO at another institution and
accepting the honest read.

### 6.6.2 The compliance self-assessment

The document reads as if it were a response to
a regulator's examination. Every section is
defensive; findings are hedged; the
recommendations are directional rather than
specific. The pattern tends to emerge when
the CAO function has absorbed too much of the
compliance posture ([`mod-109`](../mod-109-compliance-operations/README.md)
§6 is the authoritative treatment of the
CAO/compliance boundary; a self-assessment
that collapses into that posture has lost the
distinction).

The remedy is to remember the audience: the
self-assessment goes to the AIRC and the Audit
Committee, both of whom need honest judgment
to do their work. A defensive document is less
useful to them than a direct one.

### 6.6.3 The parallel self-assessment

The CAO function produces a self-assessment
that is substantively independent of the
quarterly reports and the ongoing audit trail.
The maturity ratings in the self-assessment
do not reconcile with the risk-posture ratings
in the quarterly reports; the gaps named in
the self-assessment are not reflected in the
quarterly reports' material-exceptions
section. The pattern signals that the
self-assessment is a once-a-year performance
rather than a view that integrates with the
rest of the program's reporting.

The remedy is to use the quarterly reports as
inputs to the self-assessment and to use the
self-assessment to shape the quarterly reports
in the year ahead. The two are the same view
at different cadences.

## 6.7 The CAO succession pass

An annual self-assessment is also the natural
occasion to pass the CAO succession-planning
view through to the Audit Committee. The pass
is private, outside the self-assessment
document itself, and goes to the committee
chair and the CEO. The pass covers:

- Who could cover the CAO role on short
  notice (interim capacity).
- Who is being developed for the CAO role on
  medium horizon.
- What capabilities a future CAO successor
  would need that the current function does
  not fully have.

The pass is not a public document; it is a
succession-planning input to the committee's
own talent oversight. Firms where succession
planning for the CAO role is done entirely
inside the CAO function tend to produce
succession risk that surprises the committee
when the current CAO moves on.

## Summary

- The annual self-assessment is the CAO
  function's own honest review of itself,
  distinct from internal audit (which is
  third-line and reaches the board
  separately).
- Six dimensions: program maturity, operating
  effectiveness, coverage gaps, investment
  posture, leadership and talent, cultural
  integration.
- The honesty test: would the CAO be
  comfortable showing the assessment to a
  peer CAO at a comparable institution who
  would give an honest read?
- Output: a signed document, a specific
  action list, a specific resource request.
- Annual cycle: Q4 authored, Q1 reviewed by
  AIRC and Audit Committee, Q2–Q4 actions
  tracked through quarterly reports.
- Three failure modes to avoid: the
  marketing self-assessment, the compliance
  self-assessment, the parallel self-
  assessment.
- An annual CAO succession-planning pass to
  the Audit Committee chair and CEO is the
  companion private artifact.
