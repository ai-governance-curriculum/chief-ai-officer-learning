# Chapter 5 — The CAO at the Board Table

## Why this chapter exists

The written report (Chapter 3) prepares the
room. The CAO *in* the room completes the
information transfer — answers questions,
surfaces the material the pack cannot carry,
makes the asks, handles pressure. A CAO who
writes a strong quarterly pack but performs
poorly at the table has done half the job;
a CAO who performs well at the table but
writes a weak pack has set the room up to
discount the performance.

Boards are sophisticated audiences. They read
packs they have reviewed executives through
for years; they recognise the patterns of
over-selling, under-selling, hedging, and
honest reporting. The CAO's credibility at the
table — accumulated over several cycles — is
the leverage that lets the function land the
hard asks when they come. This chapter builds
the discipline that accumulates it.

## 5.1 Where the CAO sits

Whether the CAO sits at the board table
depends on the organisation. Three common
patterns:

- **Attendee at Board Risk Committee as
  permanent invitee.** The CAO is not a
  board member but attends the Risk
  Committee as a standing invitee and
  presents the quarterly report. This is the
  most common pattern in US banks and
  insurers.
- **Formal presenter at quarterly full-board
  meetings.** The CAO presents the AI update
  at the regular full-board meeting as a
  scheduled agenda item. Common in firms
  where AI is a strategic priority rated
  high by the full board.
- **Direct relationship with the full board.**
  The CAO has standing to brief the full
  board directly on AI matters, parallel to
  the CRO or CFO relationship with the board.
  Less common; appropriate at firms where AI
  is central to strategy or where the AI
  regulatory posture is highly developed
  (large frontier-model developers;
  AI-centric financial firms).

In all three patterns, the CAO's primary
written channel is the quarterly report that
reaches the Board Risk Committee, which then
reports up to the full board. The in-room
performance is the complement, not the
substitute.

## 5.2 The CAO's role in the room

When the CAO is present at the board table,
four things:

### 5.2.1 Present the report

The report is pre-read. The CAO's presentation
is highlights and emphasis, not full read-out.
A read-out signals the CAO does not trust the
pack to have been read; the room reads this
as condescension.

A working opening: a sentence or two per
section — the posture summary, the material
exceptions, the asks. Perhaps three minutes
total. Then the floor opens for questions.

### 5.2.2 Answer questions specifically

Board questions deserve specific answers. Three
patterns to use:

- **Direct.** When the answer is known: give
  it. "The Site H-12 bias remediation is
  tracking to the June 30 commitment. We
  have shipped two of four control changes
  and are on track for the remaining two."
- **Bounded unknown.** When the specific
  answer is not known but the shape is: say
  so. "I do not have the exact customer count
  with me; it is in the low thousands. I
  will come back to the committee by Friday
  with the specific number."
- **Reasoned uncertainty.** When the answer
  depends on judgment that is genuinely
  uncertain: name the uncertainty and the
  judgment. "My view is that the regulator is
  likely to accept the remediation plan as
  submitted; the uncertainty is on the
  timing of their response, which has
  historically ranged from eight to sixteen
  weeks."

What to avoid: "I'll get back to you" as a
substitute for engaging substantively. "I'll
get back to you" is acceptable for facts
unknown but not for substantive matters where
the CAO is expected to have a view.

### 5.2.3 Surface emerging issues

The CAO is in the room as a substantive
contributor. If the board's discussion of a
non-AI matter is missing an angle the CAO can
contribute — an AI-dependency implication, a
model-risk angle, a vendor-concentration note
— the CAO should surface it.

The discipline: contribute when the AI angle
is substantive; stay silent when it is not.
CAOs who insert AI-related commentary into
every discussion are not helping; CAOs who
only speak when the AI section is on the
agenda miss opportunities to make the
function substantively useful.

### 5.2.4 Make the asks

The asks section of the written pack is
presented and the board is given the
opportunity to act. A specific ask lands
better in the room than it does on paper
because the board can respond directly:

> *"The ask is that the committee ratify the
> revised bias-monitoring threshold for
> Tier-1 systems from 5 pp to 4 pp,
> effective Q3. The AI Risk Council
> recommended the change on 2026-02-14 and
> I'm supportive. The implications are X
> and Y. I'd welcome the committee's
> view."*

Then stop talking. The silence after a
specific ask is productive silence; filling
it with elaboration dilutes the request.

## 5.3 The honesty discipline at the table

Boards' sophistication is the asset the CAO
either builds against or burns through. The
CAO's credibility depends on being honest
about what is known, what is uncertain, and
what is unknown. Three patterns to avoid:

### 5.3.1 Overclaiming

> *"Our trust architecture is fully effective
> against prompt injection."*

Almost certainly false — no architecture is
fully effective against an adversary class
that is still evolving ([`mod-106`](../mod-106-trust-architecture/README.md),
[`mod-107`](../mod-107-ai-security/README.md)).
Overclaiming has a one-time cost: the next
time the CAO reports on this topic, the board
will discount the input, and over cycles the
discount compounds. One director with an
AI-security background in the room is enough
for the overclaim to be marked.

The honest version:

> *"Our trust architecture reduces prompt
> injection exposure substantially; no
> architecture is fully effective against
> prompt injection and we treat residual risk
> explicitly in our incident playbooks."*

Both longer and more credible.

### 5.3.2 Hedging

> *"It's hard to say; it depends on several
> factors."*

The board needs a position. Hedging is a
non-answer, and the board walks away without
the input it needs.

The honest version is to name the position
and the uncertainty:

> *"My view is X. The uncertainty is Y. I'd
> be prepared to revise if Z becomes
> clearer."*

A position with named uncertainty is a
contribution. A hedge is not.

### 5.3.3 Education-as-answer

The board asks a specific question; the CAO
answers with a textbook overview of the
topic. The board walks away with no answer
to its question and the uncomfortable sense
that the CAO was performing.

The pattern often emerges when the CAO is
uncertain of the specific answer and reaches
for the familiar material. The discipline is
to resist: specific answers when you have
them; "I'll come back with specifics by
[date]" when you do not; never a textbook
substitute.

## 5.4 The disagreement discipline

When the CAO disagrees with another executive
or with a board member's framing, the CAO
must say so. The responsible-AI chapter's
treatment of naming a disagreement
([`mod-105`](../mod-105-responsible-ai-and-ethics/README.md)
§6.5) applies here directly. Three patterns:

### 5.4.1 The respectful direct

> *"I see this differently. My view is X
> because Y. I think the position you've
> outlined would lead to Z."*

Clear, specific, grounded in reasons. Boards
respect this pattern; they lose confidence in
CAOs who never disagree (signal of weak
substantive position) and in CAOs who
disagree on everything (signal of weak
collaborative posture).

### 5.4.2 The bounded

> *"I agree on most of what's been outlined
> but disagree specifically on [point].
> The alternative would be [position]."*

Partial agreement with specific disagreement.
Useful when the overall direction is right
and a specific element is not.

### 5.4.3 The provisional

> *"I want to think through this before
> committing. My initial view is X, but I'll
> come back to you by [date] with a
> considered position."*

Appropriate when the matter is substantive
and the CAO's immediate instinct does not
yet have the backing it deserves. The
provisional is not hedging — it is a
commitment to come back with a specific
position by a specific date.

What to avoid: the silent disagreement. The
CAO who disagrees but does not say so
produces a board decision taken without the
dissenting view, and the audit trail later
shows the CAO was present but silent — which
is read as agreement. Silence in the room is
assent in the record.

## 5.5 Handling pressure in the room

Board rooms produce specific pressures. Three
patterns worth preparing for:

### 5.5.1 The "aren't we overcautious" push

A director — often with a line-of-business
or product orientation — pushes back that the
appetite statement or the control posture is
too conservative; competitors are moving
faster. The response is not to defend the
posture by expanding risk discussion; it is
to name the specific trade-off:

> *"The current posture reflects a judgment
> that Y risk is not worth X capability. If
> the committee's view is that Y risk is
> acceptable in exchange for X, the appetite
> statement is the document to revise, and
> I'd be prepared to bring a revision to the
> next cycle with the trade-offs explicit."*

The response invites the board to make the
appetite choice knowingly rather than drift
into a looser posture through pressure in the
room.

### 5.5.2 The "aren't we underreacting" push

The reverse: a director — often with a risk,
audit, or compliance orientation — pushes
that the posture is too loose; the material
item should have reached the board sooner.
The response is to name the materiality
framework and the specific disposition:

> *"The matter was recognised as a boundary
> case under our materiality framework on
> [date]. The CRO was briefed on [date]
> and concurred that disposition should be
> [X]. I'll walk through the audit trail if
> that would help."*

The response grounds the disposition in a
defensible process. If the process itself
needs tightening, the committee can direct
that; if the specific call was wrong, the
committee can revise it.

### 5.5.3 The specific-case ambush

A director asks a highly specific question
about a specific case the CAO was not
expecting — a specific decision made by a
specific team, a specific vendor incident, a
specific regulatory response. The response is
to engage with what the CAO knows and name
what the CAO does not:

> *"I'm aware of the broad shape of the
> matter you're referencing. The specific
> details you've asked about I'd want to
> confirm before answering; let me come
> back to the chair by Friday with the
> specifics."*

Then follow through. Specific-case ambushes
often come from a director who has heard
about a matter outside the formal reporting
channel; the ambush is a credibility test as
much as an information request.

## 5.6 Confidentiality

Board discussions are confidential. The CAO's
ability to operate substantively at the board
table depends on never being the source of
leaks.

### 5.6.1 Internal

Even within the CAO function, board substance
is shared only on need-to-know. Verbal-only
briefings to the AI Risk Council about board
decisions are typical practice. Written
summaries that go into the audit ledger
should be scoped to what the audit record
needs, not to what the AIRC would want to
know.

### 5.6.2 Peer executives

Peer executives (CRO, CFO, CCO, GC) often ask
informally what the board thinks about a
specific matter. The answer is "I'm not at
liberty to discuss board deliberations." The
peers who hear this once rarely ask again;
peers who learn the CAO is loose with board
substance update their behaviour accordingly
— they stop including the CAO in substantive
pre-board conversations, which costs the
function.

### 5.6.3 External

Press, external counsel outside privilege,
vendors, consultants — the discipline is the
same. The CAO who is quoted in a trade
publication summarising "what the board is
thinking about AI" has made a career-level
mistake.

The exception is formal regulatory
disclosure. The CAO works with GC on what
can and must be disclosed in regulatory
filings, examination responses, and public
disclosures. Formal disclosure is not a
confidentiality violation; it is a different
discipline operating under GC's lead.

## 5.7 Building credibility over cycles

Credibility at the board table is accumulated
over cycles, not won in any one meeting.
Three patterns that compound:

- **Follow-through.** Items committed to in
  one meeting are delivered in the next. A
  director who asked a question and received
  "I'll come back by Friday" should receive
  the follow-up by Friday, in writing, with
  the specific answer. Over several cycles,
  this becomes the pattern the board uses to
  decide how much to trust the function.
- **Honest reporting of mistakes.** The
  function made a wrong materiality call; the
  function missed an obligation deadline;
  the function under-staffed a critical
  capability and the shortfall produced a
  near-miss. These things happen. Reporting
  them honestly, with the correction already
  underway, is how credibility is built.
  Hiding them is how it is lost.
- **Restraint on the ordinary.** The CAO who
  does not demand airtime in cycles when
  nothing material is happening has saved
  the airtime for cycles when something is.
  The board that sees a brief, orderly report
  in Q1 pays sharper attention when the CAO
  requests committee time in Q2.

## Summary

- The CAO's position at the board table
  varies by firm — permanent invitee to the
  Risk Committee, formal presenter at the
  full board, or direct standing with the
  full board.
- In the room: present highlights (not
  read-out), answer specifically, surface
  emerging issues when the AI angle is
  substantive, make the asks and stop
  talking.
- Honesty discipline — no overclaiming, no
  hedging, no education-as-answer. Specific
  answers when you have them; "come back by
  [date]" when you do not.
- Disagreement discipline — name the
  disagreement respectfully, directly,
  specifically. Silence in the room is
  assent in the record.
- Handle pressure by grounding in the
  appetite statement (for "overcautious"
  pushes), the materiality framework (for
  "underreacting" pushes), and by engaging
  honestly with what you do and do not know
  (for specific-case ambushes).
- Confidentiality is non-negotiable.
  Internal, peer, and external. The
  exception is formal regulatory
  disclosure under GC's lead.
- Credibility compounds over cycles.
  Follow-through, honest reporting of
  mistakes, and restraint on the ordinary
  are the three patterns that build it.
