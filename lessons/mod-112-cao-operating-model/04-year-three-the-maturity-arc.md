# Chapter 4 — Year 3: The Maturity Arc

## Why this chapter exists

Year 3 is when a program crosses from
*operating* to *operating well*. The work
shifts from doing the right things to doing
the right things efficiently and at scale.
The CAO starts spending time on strategic
contribution and external engagement rather
than on operational decisions the function
can now handle without them.

Year 3 is also when stalling becomes
visible. A program that in year 2 was still
honestly described as "building and
operating" and in year 3 is still described
the same way is stalled. The chapter maps
what year 3 should accomplish, the shift in
the CAO's own time, and the specific
warning signs the program is stalling at
year-2 levels.

## 4.1 What year 3 should accomplish

Five workstreams. Fewer than year 1 or year
2, by design — year 3 is less about new
substrates and more about operating the
substrates efficiently.

### 4.1.1 Operate efficiently

The cost per unit of risk-managed AI
decreases meaningfully. "Unit" is not
rigorously defined; the direction matters more
than the metric. Specific signals:

- Impact assessments take meaningfully less
  CAO-function time per system than in year 2.
- The audit ledger
  ([`mod-108`](../mod-108-audit-ledgers-and-evidence/README.md))
  supports evidence requests from internal
  audit and external examiners with
  materially less reconstruction work than
  year 2.
- Routine AI Review Board agenda items are
  decided in-session rather than deferred
  for study.
- Boundary disputes with MRM, CISO, and
  Compliance resolve faster because the
  decision patterns are now precedents the
  functions share.

Efficiency gains in year 3 are the dividend of
year 2's consolidation. Programs that didn't
consolidate in year 2 don't produce them in
year 3.

### 4.1.2 Scale the practice

New AI systems adopt the program's discipline
within weeks of first proposal rather than
months. The pathway from idea → impact
assessment → tier assignment → control
specification → launch review is now a
pathway the business units know how to walk
without CAO-function hand-holding.

A specific marker: the function stops
authoring most of the impact assessments and
starts reviewing them. The business units
author, the function reviews. This is the
difference between the function being a
*service* (I write your impact assessment) and
a *governance body* (I review the impact
assessment you wrote). Year 3 crosses that
line.

### 4.1.3 Establish industry voice

The CAO and the program have something to
contribute to the industry conversation. Not
marketing — substantive contribution to
practitioner bodies (GARP, IIA, AICPA
engagements), regulator public comment
proceedings (CFPB rulemakings, NIST AI RMF
revisions, EU AI Act implementing acts),
and peer-institution dialogue.

The purpose is not visibility; it is
*calibration*. A function that engages only
internally eventually drifts from the
industry's shared discipline. A function that
engages externally learns what peers are
seeing and where the industry's practice is
heading.

### 4.1.4 Build leadership succession

The CAO is no longer the single load-bearing
executive. The Deputy / Deputy AI Risk Lead
or equivalent (hired in year 2) can
substantively run the function for weeks at
a time. Direct reports can present to the
Board Risk Committee without the CAO
rehearsing them line by line.

Succession is the subject of Chapter 6 in
detail. The year-3 marker is specifically
that the function is **continuously
succession-ready**, not that a successor is
being specifically groomed. The two are
different: the second is CAO-personal; the
first is a function property.

### 4.1.5 Pursue strategic ambition

The CAO contributes to organisational strategy
beyond defensive risk management. This can
mean:

- Shaping the organisation's AI investment
  posture at the executive-committee level,
  not just governing the investments after
  they are made.
- Participating in M&A diligence on
  AI-dependent targets.
- Partnering with the Chief Strategy Officer
  or CEO on AI-enabled capability roadmaps.
- Representing the organisation externally
  on AI policy positions.

The strategic contribution is not a replacement
for governance — the CAO still owns the
governance discipline. It is an *addition* that
mature programs can sustain and immature
programs cannot. CAOs who try to pursue
strategic ambition before year 3 usually do so
at the expense of the governance work.

## 4.2 The shift in the CAO's own time

In year 3, the CAO should spend materially
less time on operational decisions and
materially more on:

- **Strategic contribution.** As §4.1.5.
- **Board and executive relationship-building.**
  Outside-the-meeting conversations with
  directors and executive peers. Chapter 5 §5.2.
- **Talent development.** Direct reports and
  cross-functional partners — not just hiring
  but growing. Chapter 6 §6.5.
- **External engagement.** Regulators, industry
  bodies, peer-CAO network. Chapter 5.
- **Reading and reflection.** The pace of
  development in AI governance is enough that
  a CAO who stopped reading at year 1 is
  materially behind by year 3.

The shift requires deputies who can carry the
operational load. The Deputy AI Risk Lead
role identified in year 2 is specifically
there to make the year-3 time-shift possible.

CAOs who do not shift their own time in year
3 become bottlenecks. Every operational
decision continuing to require their personal
involvement means every decision waits for
their calendar. The function at year 3 can
and should operate past that constraint; a
CAO who prevents it is stalling the function
even as they describe it as succeeding.

## 4.3 Warning signs of stalling

Programs that do not reach year-3 maturity
show specific symptoms in year 3. The honest
CAO self-assessment surfaces at least one.

### 4.3.1 CAO still personally handling many operational decisions

The CAO is in the critical path for routine
impact assessment approvals, routine tier
assignments, routine vendor exceptions, routine
incident classifications. The function hired
leads to handle these in year 1 and year 2;
if the CAO is still handling them in year 3,
either the leads are under-authorised or the
CAO has not released authority.

### 4.3.2 New AI systems take months to enter the program

The pathway §4.1.2 describes exists on paper
but takes months in practice. New AI systems
sit in queue; impact assessments take
quarters; tier decisions get escalated to the
AI Risk Council because the function has not
built decision confidence at the operational
level.

### 4.3.3 Still building foundational artifacts

The policy hierarchy still has load-bearing
documents in draft. The incident response
playbook has sections marked "to be
specified". The audit ledger coverage is still
expanding to year-1 systems. Artifacts that
year 2 should have completed are still being
built, which means year 3 has not started
operating on them.

### 4.3.4 Board reporting still has "look at what we built" quality

Board reports still spend most of their
pages on new infrastructure stood up. The
posture is still *investment* rather than
*operation*. A board at year 3 that is still
hearing primarily about new infrastructure is
being told, implicitly, that the program is
not yet operating — and the board is likely
to start asking why.

### 4.3.5 First material incident has not surfaced

Most programs face a first material incident
in year 2. A year-3 program that has had no
material incident is either exceptionally
fortunate or is **under-detecting**. The
posture to take is specifically the second:
if year 2 and year 3 have produced no
incidents of material consequence, the trust
architecture
([`mod-106`](../mod-106-trust-architecture/README.md))
is probably not calibrated sensitively enough
and the incident classification taxonomy
([`mod-110`](../mod-110-incident-response/README.md)
§3) is probably setting the threshold too
high. "No incidents" at year 3 is a finding
to investigate, not a trophy to celebrate.

### 4.3.6 Pattern

A CAO who recognises two or more of these
symptoms in year 3 should treat them as a
signal that the program's maturation has
stalled. The right posture is to engage the
AI Risk Council and the Board Risk Committee
specifically on what is needed — more
investment, structural change, hiring
re-prioritisation — rather than continuing
to describe the program as "building". The
candour is uncomfortable in the moment and
is what preserves the function's credibility
over the next several years.

## 4.4 The CAO personally at year 3

A CAO in their third year in role looks
materially different from a CAO in their first
year. The differences:

- **More authoritative in cross-functional
  conversations.** Three years of specific
  cases discussed with the CRO, CCO, CISO,
  CFO, GC produces shared vocabulary and
  mutual credibility. The year-3 CAO can
  disagree with peers more productively than
  the year-1 CAO could.
- **More selective about what reaches them
  personally.** The year-1 CAO handles every
  consequential case personally because the
  function is small. The year-3 CAO handles
  only the cases that specifically require
  the CAO altitude. Delegation (Chapter 5
  §5.3) is a year-3 maturity marker.
- **More strategic in board engagements.** The
  year-1 CAO is proving the function belongs
  at the board table. The year-3 CAO is
  shaping which conversations the board has
  about AI, not just reporting on the
  function's work. See
  [`mod-111`](../mod-111-board-reporting/README.md)
  Chapter 5 on the CAO at the board table.
- **More credibly able to disagree with peers.**
  Per
  [`mod-105`](../mod-105-responsible-ai-and-ethics/README.md)
  §6.5 and
  [`mod-111`](../mod-111-board-reporting/README.md)
  §5.3 — naming disagreements cleanly is
  structurally easier when the CAO has three
  years of operating record behind them.

The personal shift accompanies the program
shift. CAOs who do not shift personally as
the program matures become the bottleneck in
their own function. Being personally
irreplaceable *is* the stalling pattern.

## 4.5 The year-3 self-assessment

The year-3 self-assessment is mature. It
produces specific findings about what the
program does well, specific findings about
what remains fragile (see Capstone §4 in
Exercise 05), specific improvement
commitments for year 4, and candid
next-frontier questions.

The peer-CAO honesty test from
[`mod-111`](../mod-111-board-reporting/README.md)
§6.3 becomes easier in year 3 — the program
has enough operating history to support
honest assessment. A year-3 self-assessment
that reads as self-congratulatory is almost
always under-stressing some fragility that
year 4 will surface the hard way.

A specific marker: the year-3 self-assessment
names at least one *strategic* finding — a
direction the function is choosing to pursue
or decline, not just an operational finding.
The strategic finding is the signal that the
CAO has moved from managing the function to
shaping it.

## Summary

- Year 3 shifts from operating to operating
  well. Five workstreams — efficient
  operation, scale, industry voice,
  leadership succession, strategic ambition.
- The CAO's own time shifts in year 3 toward
  strategy, board and executive relationships,
  talent, external engagement, and reading.
  CAOs who do not shift personally stall the
  function.
- Stalling symptoms: CAO still handling
  operational decisions; new systems take
  months to enter the program; foundation
  artifacts still in draft; "look what we
  built" board reporting; no material
  incident at year 3 (likely under-detection).
- The year-3 CAO is more authoritative, more
  selective, more strategic, and more
  credibly able to disagree than the year-1
  CAO. The personal shift accompanies the
  program shift.
- The year-3 self-assessment is specific,
  honest, and should contain at least one
  strategic finding — not just operational
  findings.
