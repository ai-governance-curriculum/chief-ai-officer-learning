# Chapter 5 — The CAO's Calendar and Decision Rhythm

## Why this chapter exists

The CAO's calendar is the function's organising
chassis. A CAO whose calendar is driven by
everyone else's priorities does not have time
for the structural work the preceding chapters
describe — the appetite statement does not get
drafted deliberately, the standards do not get
authored carefully, the deputies do not get
developed, the board relationship is not built
outside the quarterly meeting.

The pattern that separates effective CAOs from
exhausted ones is not working harder. It is
operating the calendar deliberately.

This chapter maps the recurring rhythm a
working CAO calendar needs, the specific time
blocks that have to be protected, the
delegation discipline that frees the CAO's
time, and the crisis discipline that stops
incidents from permanently colonising the
calendar.

The content is adjacent to Andrew Grove's
*High Output Management* and Henry Mintzberg's
work on managerial work; the specific
instantiation for the CAO role is this
chapter's addition.

## 5.1 The recurring rhythm

A working CAO calendar has activities at
multiple cadences. The specific items vary by
organisation; the pattern of cadences is
stable.

| Cadence | Representative activities |
|---|---|
| Daily | Standup with AI Risk Lead (15 min); review of any overnight incidents or escalations |
| Weekly | 1:1 with each direct report; review of AI Review Board agenda; weekly bias / drift / abuse monitoring summary review |
| Bi-weekly | AI Review Board (chair); business-unit head 1:1 rotation |
| Monthly | AI Risk Council (chair); 1:1 with CRO, CCO, CISO, MRM Lead; peer-CAO call at a non-competing institution |
| Quarterly | Board Risk Committee meeting; AI Risk Council strategic review; CAO's own quarterly self-review |
| Annually | Annual program self-assessment per [`mod-111`](../mod-111-board-reporting/README.md) Chapter 6; annual board adoption review of the appetite statement; annual external benchmarking against peers and NIST AI RMF |

The calendar is **crowded** at the CAO
altitude; the role is senior enough that
substantial recurring commitments are
unavoidable. The calendar should also be
**deliberate** — each recurring item has a
specific purpose, a specific owner (not
necessarily the CAO), and a specific
*output*.

Items on the calendar that cannot be mapped
to a specific output are candidates for
elimination. A standing meeting without a
specific output is a meeting that will drift
in substance and consume calendar capacity
the function needs for other work.

## 5.2 Time protection

A working CAO protects specific time blocks
for work the operational flow would otherwise
crowd out:

### 5.2.1 Strategic thinking

Half a day per week, blocked, undisturbed.
The specific purpose is work that requires
sustained attention — reading, writing, the
year-ahead planning, the harder appetite-
statement revisions. Half-days broken into
30-minute segments produce none of the
reflection the block exists for.

The specific signal that strategic thinking
time is being protected: the CAO can point to
a *written artifact* that only exists because
the time was protected. If the block is
notionally on the calendar but produces no
artifact, it was not actually protected.

### 5.2.2 Direct report development

Weekly 1:1s treated as inviolable. The 1:1
is the single most leveraged form of
management work the CAO does; cancelling
1:1s to accommodate other priorities signals
the direct report that their development is
subordinate to whatever cancelled it. See
Andrew Grove's *High Output Management* for
the foundational treatment.

The specific disciplines:

- 1:1s happen on the calendar even when the
  CAO or the direct report would rather
  skip them.
- 1:1s are not status updates — the direct
  report brings the agenda.
- 1:1s are rescheduled if cancelled, not
  dropped.

### 5.2.3 Stakeholder relationship-building

Outside-the-fire-drill conversations with
the CRO, CCO, CISO, MRM Lead, CFO, GC. The
pattern is often a rolling monthly 1:1
rotation — one of these per week on a four-
week cycle — but the specific pattern varies.

The purpose is not operational; the
operational conversations happen in the
AI Risk Council and in incident response. The
purpose is relationship capital. The CAO
who only engages peers transactionally
discovers in year 2 that peers do not
support them when a hard decision needs
cross-functional backing. The CAO who
invests in relationships ahead of needing
them has peers who back them in year 2 and
year 3.

### 5.2.4 External engagement

Regulators, industry bodies, peer CAOs. A
rolling 6-week calendar of external
engagements — one meeting with a regulator,
one with an industry body, one with a peer
CAO, in rotation — keeps the CAO calibrated
externally without consuming excessive time.

Year-1 external engagement is light; year 3
external engagement is substantial. The
calendar progression should match.

### 5.2.5 Protection discipline per block

Each protected block has three disciplines:

- **The block.** What time, what duration, how
  often.
- **The conditions under which it can be
  interrupted.** Few. For the strategic
  thinking block, typically only a
  materiality-threshold incident or a direct
  board request. For the 1:1, almost nothing.
- **The recovery pattern after disruption.**
  When the block is interrupted, when it is
  rescheduled. A block that is interrupted
  and not rescheduled is a block that no
  longer exists.

The recovery discipline is as important as
the protection discipline. Blocks that are
protected absolutely but never recovered
after disruption produce the same result as
blocks that were never protected.

## 5.3 Delegation

A specific year-1-and-2 discipline:
delegating work that the CAO does not need
to personally do. The temptation is to do
everything because the team is small and the
work is consequential. The function cannot
scale on that posture.

The default delegation pattern:

- **The AI Risk Lead** handles operational
  incident response for all but the most
  material cases. The CAO engages when the
  incident reaches a material threshold
  (per
  [`mod-110`](../mod-110-incident-response/README.md)
  §3) or when the response needs cross-
  functional escalation beyond the Lead's
  authority.
- **The Policy Lead** owns standard
  authorship. The CAO reviews and sharpens;
  the Policy Lead writes.
- **The Evidence Lead** owns the audit ledger
  and the continuous-evidence cadence per
  [`mod-108`](../mod-108-audit-ledgers-and-evidence/README.md)
  and
  [`mod-109`](../mod-109-compliance-operations/README.md).
  The CAO reviews patterns surfaced; the
  Evidence Lead operates the substrate.
- **The Regulatory Engagement Lead** owns
  routine regulator interface. The CAO
  engages on material regulator conversations;
  the Lead owns everything else.

The CAO personally handles:

- Strategic decisions and strategic contribution.
- Executive relationships (CRO, CCO, CISO,
  CFO, GC, CEO).
- Board engagement and the quarterly report
  authorship.
- Material incidents' CAO-level moments —
  the notification matrix calls, the specific
  decisions that need CAO altitude, the
  post-incident review participation.
- The small number of boundary cases where
  the CAO's authority is specifically needed.

The discipline is naming what gets delegated
*in advance* rather than reactively. A CAO
who delegates reactively (I'll do this
because it's urgent, I'll delegate it next
time) ends up delegating little. A CAO who
delegates by charter — the Policy Lead owns
standard authorship, full stop — frees the
time the role needs.

## 5.4 The crisis discipline

When an incident or crisis arrives, the CAO
is pulled into operations. This is
appropriate; material incidents are
CAO-level events. The crisis discipline is
*getting back to the recurring rhythm
afterward*.

Three specific patterns:

### 5.4.1 Named "fire mode"

The function names explicitly when it is in
fire mode. Not "we're busy this week" —
"we are in fire mode for incident X, which
means the following standing meetings are
suspended, the following external
engagements deferred, and we will reconvene
the normal cadence on date Y". The naming
disciplines the exit.

### 5.4.2 Fire mode has an end

A specific date, published, after which the
function reverts to normal cadence. The
date can slip if the incident continues to
require it, but the slip is explicit. Fire
modes without end dates become permanent
operating states, which is how CAO calendars
drift from the deliberate rhythm §5.1
describes into pure reactivity.

### 5.4.3 Calendar reset half-day

After the immediate crisis phase, schedule a
"calendar reset" half-day within 2 weeks to
restore the protected time blocks §5.2
named, catch up on cancelled 1:1s, and
check in with external relationships that
were deferred during fire mode.

The reset half-day is the specific artifact
of the crisis-discipline pattern. A CAO who
can point to a scheduled reset half-day on
their calendar within 2 weeks of a resolved
incident has the discipline; a CAO who
cannot does not.

## 5.5 The practice patterns

The patterns that separate effective CAOs
from exhausted ones are not surprising. They
are the practice patterns the leadership
literature has described for decades:

- **Deliberate calendar over reactive.** The
  calendar reflects the function's priorities,
  not the inbound requests' priorities.
- **Protected blocks for the work that gets
  crowded out.** Strategic thinking, direct
  report development, stakeholder
  relationships, external engagement.
- **Delegation by charter over delegation by
  availability.** The function's division of
  labour is declared and sustained.
- **Fire mode is named and bounded.** Crises
  do not permanently colonise the calendar.
- **1:1s are inviolable.** The single most
  leveraged form of management work.
- **Reading time is protected.** The pace of
  development in AI governance makes stale
  CAOs ineffective quickly.

None of these are new disciplines. The CAO
role is new; the operating discipline it
needs is old. CAOs who treat the practice
patterns as optional learn in year 2 or
year 3 that they were not.

## Summary

- The CAO's calendar has recurring activity
  at daily, weekly, bi-weekly, monthly,
  quarterly, and annual cadences. Each item
  has a specific output.
- Four protected time blocks: strategic
  thinking (half-day/week), direct report
  development (weekly 1:1s inviolable),
  stakeholder relationships (monthly
  rotation), external engagement (rolling
  6-week).
- Each protected block has an explicit
  discipline of protection and an explicit
  recovery pattern. Blocks that aren't
  recovered after disruption no longer exist.
- Delegation is declared in advance by
  charter — the AI Risk Lead handles
  operational incident response; the Policy
  Lead owns standard authorship; the
  Evidence Lead owns the ledger; the
  Regulatory Engagement Lead owns the
  routine regulator interface.
- The crisis discipline names fire mode
  explicitly, bounds it with an end date,
  and schedules a calendar reset half-day
  within 2 weeks after resolution.
- The practice patterns that separate
  effective CAOs from exhausted ones are not
  new. They are classical leadership
  disciplines applied to the specific CAO
  cadence.
